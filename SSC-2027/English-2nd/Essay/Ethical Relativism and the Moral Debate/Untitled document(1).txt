 Cultural relativism and moral relativism are essential frameworks for understanding the diversity of moral beliefs across societies. Cultural relativism asserts that moral standards are determined by cultural norms. Each society develops its own rules, values, and practices based on history, religion, and tradition. For instance, some cultures prioritize family obligations or arranged marriages, while others emphasize personal choice in social and moral matters. Cultural relativism encourages observers to evaluate these practices in context without imposing external moral standards.
Moral relativism, in contrast, emphasizes that morality is not universal. Right and wrong are determined by the beliefs of individuals or communities rather than fixed principles. For example, certain dietary practices or gender roles may be morally acceptable in one society but disapproved of in another. Moral relativism highlights the subjectivity of ethical judgment, underscoring that ethical reasoning is contingent on perspective rather than absolute rules.
While related, cultural relativism and moral relativism are distinct. Cultural relativism focuses on societal influence, whereas moral relativism stresses individual or collective moral judgment. This distinction is particularly important when analyzing historical atrocities, such as the persecution of Jews and homosexuals in Nazi Germany. Understanding both perspectives allows for a more nuanced ethical analysis that recognizes cultural and ideological influence while still addressing the limits of relativism. Even though culture shapes moral reasoning, some actions may require universal ethical condemnation.
Many people think that the fact that people disagree about morality shows that ethical relativism must be true  –  the idea that moral truths have no universal standard and depend on culture, time, or perspective.  Ethical relativism argues that right and wrong vary from one society to another, and there is no single moral standard for everyone.  According to this view, if one culture says X is wrong and another says it is right, then both are correct relative to their own context.  This idea seems plausible because different moral practices and beliefs indeed exist across cultures and historical periods  (Encyclopaedia Britannica Editors, 2026).  


However, many philosophers argue that disagreement alone does not prove that ethical relativism is true.  Critics point out that people can disagree about many things, even in fields like science, but this does not mean there is no objective truth behind those disagreements  (Rachels, n.d.).  For example, people once disagreed about the causes of disease, but this disagreement did not negate the existence of an underlying biological fact about microbes  –  scientists eventually discovered the truth.  In the same way, ethical disagreements may reflect differences in understanding or context rather than the absence of moral truth  (Encyclopaedia Britannica Editors, 2026).  


My view is that morals and ethics depend on time, place, and historical context because nothing in human culture is permanent.  Moral beliefs and traditions evolve as societies learn, adapt, and reinterpret their values.  People change their moral perspectives over time as they encounter new challenges and experiences, showing that morality is dynamic rather than static.

Human nature, culture, and personality are related but different concepts that help us understand human behavior.  Human nature refers to the universal traits shared by all humans, like basic emotions, survival instincts, and the ability to feel empathy.  These traits are not influenced by where or when someone is born; they are part of what makes us human.  
Culture, on the other hand, is shaped by the traditions, beliefs, and practices of a particular society.  Culture determines what people consider acceptable behavior, rituals, moral values, and social norms.  For example, some cultures emphasize collective responsibility, while others value individual achievement.  Culture is learned and transmitted through socialization, education, and experience rather than inherited biologically.  
Personality is about the unique characteristics and behavior patterns of an individual.  Even people within the same culture can think, feel, and act very differently from one another.  Personality includes traits like introversion, creativity, patience, or impulsiveness, which make each person distinct.  
These three concepts are separate because they operate on different levels.  Human nature provides the foundation of what all humans are capable of.  Culture influences what is considered right or normal within a society.  Personality determines how a person interprets, reacts to, and sometimes even challenges their culture.  For instance, two siblings raised in the same family with the same cultural norms may still develop very different personalities, and these differences can affect how they follow or resist cultural expectations.  Understanding the separation between human nature, culture, and personality helps us explain why people sometimes behave in unexpected ways and why societies are diverse, even though all humans share fundamental traits.
During World War II, the regime in Nazi Germany carried out one of the most systematic and horrific campaigns of persecution and genocide in human history.  The Nazi government, led by Adolf Hitler, believed that “inferior” groups such as Jews, Roma, people with disabilities, homosexuals, and others threatened the racial purity of the so‑called Aryan nation.  This was rooted in extreme racial ideology that claimed Germans were superior to other groups and that those labeled as “subhuman” should be removed or eliminated to protect the German “master race” (Encyclopaedia Britannica Editors, 2026; United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, n.d.).  
The Holocaust refers specifically to the systematic, state‑sponsored persecution and murder of approximately six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators between 1939 and 1945, in addition to millions of other victims who were also targeted because of who they were or how they were defined by Nazi racial laws (National WWII Museum, n.d.; Encyclopaedia Britannica Editors, 2026).  Homosexuals were persecuted under laws that treated them as threats to Nazi social norms and racial purity, and many were imprisoned or killed (Encyclopaedia Britannica Editors, 2026; Holocaust Education Centre, n.d.).  
If ethical relativism is taken to mean that moral judgments depend entirely on cultural norms and that outsiders should not judge another society’s moral code, then from that perspective one might argue that Nazi Germany’s actions could not be criticized by those outside the regime.  Ethical relativists claim morality is a product of culture, so moral judgment from the outside has no standing.  However, genocide — the deliberate killing of millions of innocent people — seems to violate basic ethical principles that many cultures and moral theories hold universally.  Acts of mass murder, discrimination, and systematic oppression transcend cultural norms and appear morally unacceptable regardless of context.  Therefore, while cultural context influences moral viewpoints, ethical relativism alone cannot justify actions like genocide, and many philosophers argue that some moral standards must be universal in order to condemn such extreme harm.
In conclusion, this essay has examined how culture shapes moral beliefs and how ethical relativism can help explain why different societies hold different views about right and wrong.  Cultural context clearly influences how people perceive ethical actions, and moral standards often reflect the values, traditions, and historical experiences of a society.  Ethical relativism helps us understand disagreements across cultures and why people might justify actions differently.  However, the examples of extreme cases such as the Holocaust demonstrate the limits of ethical relativism.  Even if morality is culturally determined, some acts like genocide appear universally wrong because they violate fundamental human dignity and cause immense harm.  
Ultimately, nothing in human nature or society is static.  Over time, viewpoints, beliefs, cultures, and traditions change, and moral principles evolve alongside historical events and social developments.  Therefore, moral judgment depends not only on culture but also on time, place, and context, showing that ethical reflection must balance cultural understanding with universal concerns about human well-being.
